## The naive way breaks
Checking the distance from you to every car in a city on every request is O(n) per request — too slow and too expensive at Uber's scale.

## H3: the world as hexagons
Uber built **H3**, a hexagonal hierarchical spatial index, and open-sourced it in 2018 ([Uber Engineering, 27 Jun 2018](https://www.uber.com/blog/h3/)).
- Every location maps to a **cell ID** at a chosen resolution (16 resolutions; each finer cell ≈ 1/7 the area).
- A car's GPS point → its cell ID, stored in an index keyed by cell.
- **Why hexagons:** all six neighbours are the same distance from the centre (squares have two neighbour distances), which makes "nearby" and smoothing simple.

## Search with rings (k-ring)
1. Look up cars in **your cell**.
2. Not enough? Add the **6 neighbours** (ring 1), then ring 2…
3. Stop as soon as you have enough candidates, then rank them by **ETA** (road distance, not straight line).

Uber also measures **supply and demand per hexagon** — that's where surge pricing comes from.

## The interview answer: geospatial index
- Index by **geohash / H3 / S2 cell**, not raw lat-lng.
- Query = cell + neighbour rings (handle cell edges!).
- Keep the index **in memory**, sharded by region; drivers update every few seconds.
- Rank candidates by ETA from the routing service.

**Words to say:** geospatial index, geohash/H3, k-ring, sharding by region, ETA ranking.
